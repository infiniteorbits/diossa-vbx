Introduction
============

LMO in partnership with SnT (University of Luxembourg) are carrying out
the Development of In-Orbit Servicing Space Situational Awareness
payloads using deep learning (DIOSSA) in the framework of an ESA project
(contract No. 4000136351/21/NL/VR). The RPO system is a suite of
advanced vision-based navigation technologies for autonomous rendezvous
and proximity operations, using deep learning computer vision algorithms
on edge. It autonomously derives the 6 degrees-of-freedom pose
estimation of a target space resident object under any illumination
condition and is part of the spacecraft GNC system. The payload is
composed of several monocular cameras, the control and vision processing
electronics, and the software. The objective of this project is the
design, development, verification and qualification of the RPO system
for space application.

Objective
---------

The objective of this document is to present the rationale, parameters,
assumptions and models used to develop the DIOSSA RPO system.

Sensor selection
================

The visible spectrum camera has been selected in the first phase of the DIOSSA project. 

The thermal infrared camera from MDA has been selected based on the following aspects:

- Detector selection and performance: MDA camera uses the PICO1024 microbolometer version
  (originally developed by ULIS, currently Lynred) which has alraedy gone through an extensive
  qualification campaign conducted with ESA (see [RD-2]).
  The camera features a 17um pixel pitch which, as far as LMO current knowledge goes at the moment
  of writing this, has the best performance in the market for space capable microbolometers, both
  in terms of sensitivity, resolution and robustness.
- Maturity: MDA has produced already flight models of the camera, confirming a maturity higher than
  TRL7 and MDA capacity to deliver such payload. Although not much flight heritage data is avaliable
  at the moment, it is expected to become available shortly.

Reliability and Quality assurance aspects have not been taken into consideration yet in this selection,
because LMO hasn't reached that level of engagement with the supplier at this stage of the project.

The statements of compliance for the 2 cameras (VIS and TIR) are provided in annex.


Mechanical design
=================

As opposed to previous design, LMO and ESA have agreed to have a new design with the computer 
separated from the optics.

As the computer is procured with its enclosure, only the Optical Head Unit (OHU)
needs to be designed, with the following assumptions:

- the baseline is 1 VIS and 1 TIR camera
- the structure serves both as mechanical and thermal interface
- the interface between the OHU structure and the spacecraft structure
  must be on the same side as the interfaces between the cameras and the
  OHU structure (corresponding to the camera openings)
- the structure includes a reference point visible when
  the OHU is mounted on the spacecraft that can be used for alignment measurement

Structural and thermal analysis
===============================

As part of the DIOSSA Payload, LMO has incorporated the SpectraCAM visual camera from Redwire
and the Infrared SIRC camera from MDA. For the electronics the baseline is that this will be
mounted together with the rest of the platform electronics and not as part of the payload optical head.
They will therefore not be considered as part of this assessment.

As there are not yet clear defined platform requirements from an environmental perspective for the
GEA RISE or ENCORE Mission LMO will define the constraints of the DIOSSA Payload based on the
qualification environments of the key components of its system which are the two cameras. 

The following chapter reviews the mechanical and thermal constraints of this design.

Overall Design
--------------

The current design expects the sensors to be integrated via a frame in which the cameras are mounted.
The frame design will directly connect to the satellite structure with no secondary structures to mount
the cameras on. The frame is baselined to be designed in aluminium.

Based on this the frame will have no impact on the thermal or structural behaviour of the payload.
It is therefore assumed that the interface of the cameras with the frame can be is identical to them
being interfaced with the platform.

This means that the DIOSSA payload requirements will be set based on the constraints defined by each camera.
In future LMO will work with the customer to ensure that either the customer meets the dedicated environmental
constraints of the Payload (i.e. the cameras) or that a specific design/delta-qualification is done.

Synthetic data generation
=========================

The synthetic images were generated in PANGU, according to the mission specifications, i.e. target satellite, operational range, orbital approach and sensor characteristics. The major change compared to DIOSSA phase 1 is that the target satellite is Inmarsat-5, orbiting in GEO (instead of OneWeb in LEO). Another change is the candidate approach trajectories, which now start from a distance of 100m with a flyaround at 20m and end at 2m.  

In light of this information, we could define the boundary conditions for the synthetic data generation. For the training dataset, we generated 32400 images, randomly sampled from 225 attitudes on the Fibonacci lattice, from 12 distances from 12 Gaussian distributions and from 12 sun positions, again following the Fibonacci lattice.
The validation dataset was generated based on the trajectories provided by the GNC subsystem and were not used for training. There were 7 candidate approach trajectories, each with approximately 7000 image, a total of 48635 images. 

Regarding the satellite modelling, the 3D model designed by LMO, based on publicly available information of the satellite, such as the dimensions, the solar panels, photographs and materials. We have created a process of importing a satellite's CAD model into PANGU and using that model to generate synthetic images (more details in the Synthetic Data Package [AD-1] documentation).

The modelling of the camera in PANGU followed the specifications of the actual camera used in the lab campaign, with the same resolution and intrinsic parameters as defined by the calibration process. 


Algorithm selection
===================


Experiment campaign plan
------------------------

An *experiment* in this context refers to training, inference and validation of a given task model with all of its hyperparameters. A *task model* refers to a machine learning model that solves a particular task, for example: object detection or keypoint regression. Furthermore, hyperparameters include but are not limited to: model selection (FCOS, FasterRCNN, etc), learning rate and scheduler, batch size, loss function.


Task models
^^^^^^^^^^^

In the context of DIOSSA CCN phase, we have 2 task models:

- **Object Detection** - a model that finds a predefined object in the field of view and outputs its location in form of a rectangular bounding box (aligned to the horizontal line) and a prediction score in the domain [0, 1]
- **Keypoint Regression** - a model that finds `n` predefined keypoints in the image that correspond to `n` 3d points on the given 3-D model (eg: a satellite). Not all `n` keypoints might be in the field of view and not all might be in direct line of sight (some might be occluded by the object itself). The model outputs `n` 2d locations (x,y) coordinates and `n` `MxM` 2d heatmaps that correspond to the probability density of the corresponding keypoints.

Metrics
^^^^^^^

We have fixed the following metrics for the task models:

- Object detection:
    - **Primary:** IoU (Intersection-over-Union)
    - **Secondary:** mAP (mean average precision, tightly correlated in this project due to 1 object in the scene)
- Keypoint Regression:
    - **Primary:** L1 error on all keypoints
    - **Support:** Average score
    - **Secondary Support:** Average covariance components (xx,xy,yy)

Hyper-parameter search space
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Hyper-parameters are parameters that are not optimized during the training itself, that is during back-propagation. They are instead set before training begins and remain the same for the duration of the training run. These include for example image input size, number of keypoints, learning rate scheduling strategy, etc.

Transfer learning
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Transfer learning refers to using the knowledge learned by a neural network on a similar task to boost the performance, significantly reduce training time and required data on your desired task. For example, for satellite object detection we can apply transfer learning by using an object detection model that was trained on the COCO dataset, which contains 80 different objects. Here are transfer learning strategies, we are considering: 

- **Object detection:**
    - Start training from scratch (randomly initialized weights, no transfer learning)
    - Start training from the backbone pretrained on ImageNet or similar, where the rest is initialized to random weights
    - Start training from COCO dataset
- **Keypoint regression:**
    - Start training from scratch (randomly initialized weights, no transfer learning)
    - Start training from the backbone pretrained on ImageNet or similar, where the rest is initialized to random weights
    - Start training from HumanPose dataset (predicts locations of human joints to estimate the human pose)
    - Start training from one of the best performing models used in DIOSSA-1 

Static image processing
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
We refer to *static image processing* by all the image processing that is always applied to image and does not change from an image to image. That includes image cropping and/or padding, resizing, undistorting, autocropping, etc. It does **not** include any data augmentation, which would only be used during training.

The search space of all possible center crops and resizes is of course infinite and choosing too many possible scenarios will lead to a combinatorial explosion of number of experiments needed to run. Therefore, we have decided that we will proceed only with 3 rough size categories as the task model input sizes:

**Keypoint Regression:**
    - large [448x448]
    - medium [224x224]
    - small [112x112]

**Object Detection:**
    - large [640x480]
    - medium [384x288]
    - small [224x224]

.. note:: Object detection task model

    The object detection task model would suffer from cropping too much. Therefore the model will accept a rectangular image that deviates as little as possible from the raw camera image in terms of aspect ratio and is still a valid input size (some architectures have requirements on aspect ratios, height and width)

.. note:: Keypoint regression task model

    The keypoint regression model produces heatmaps that are proportional the the input image size. For MobilePose, that ratio is 1/4, ie for a `224x224` input image the output heatmap size is `56x56`. Larger heatmap sizes should in theory provide better accuracy but it remains to be seen. Furthermore, this model is trained on ideal object locations meaning that we crop the image to remove a lot of unnecessary information and avoid losing signal during downsizing. Assuming the object detection model is a perfect detector, the expansion ratio (the padding added to the bounding box) added before cropping can be minimal or zero. Therefore, we can start with a fixed expansion ratio of 5-10 %, but decrease it if we find that the object detection model performs very well in order to increase the accuracy of the keypoint regression task model

Things that still need considering:

- Pixel depth: 8, 12, 16 or other ? Does it have an impact if we are quantizing the model to 8bit precision.
- Number of channels; Would it make sense to train on gray-scale images ?

Data augmentation
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
Data augmentation is a technique that should lead to a better generalization of the task model. Its goal is to disturb images by adding different kinds of noise in order to learn a better internal representation of the images that will lead to a better generalization across the domain gap (synthetic to lab to space). At least that's our hypothesis. It has proven itself empirically to be a valuable tool in achieving a greater performance on a variety of machine learning tasks.

We will follow the data augmentation approach from D'Amico paper [RD-3] and might need to revise the approach if the performance is still lacking. However, since we are not trying to close the domain gap in DIOSSA-CCN1, we have decided not to use data augmentation in this model training iteration.

Other typical hyperparameters
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
The remaining typical (but not less important!) hyperparameters are:
- Optimizer (Adam, RMSProp, SGD, etc)
- Learning rate
- Learning rate schedules
- L2/drop-out regularization
- Number of epochs
- Loss function specifics:

    - MobilePose has a sigma parameter for JS heatmap loss that can be changed
    - Constrain loss function to work only on visible keypoints
    - Loss function components can have weighting factors

- Batch size, usually set to highest possible that still fits inside your GPU

Some of the above parameters have a massive impact on the stability of the training, eg learning rate and some have less. Finding them is usually done through trail-and-error process or doing a grid/random search. There exist some method on performing a more guided search through the hyperparameter space, though it is still immature and largely academic.

The general approach 
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

- We train and evaluate each task model separately; no end-to-end pose estimation evaluation at this stage
- We train only on synthetic data, evaluation on synthetic and on lab data
- From all the possible hyper-parameters, we should classify them as first and second class citizens:
    - First class citizens: We experiment first with those that we expect to have the highest impact. After the first round of experiments we freeze them and continue with the rest in a second round. These could be:
        - Learning from scratch vs Transfer learning 
        - No data augmentation vs various data augmentation strategies
        - Learning rate and scheduling
        - Static image processing (image sizes: small/medium/large)
    - Second class citizens: We target to fine-tune an already well designed experiment from the previous round. For example:
        - Train with or without L2 regularization and/or dropout
        - Train using all keypoints vs using only visible keypoints
        - Train with all 20 keypoints or a subset (maybe 10?)
        - Train with different loss functions and/or weighting
        - Training epochs

Note on quantization
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
Quantization is necessary for deployment to edge devices, which do not have floating point operations. We refer to these models as *quantized*, whereas *float* models are models trained and evaluated on the GPU/CPU, which **cannot** be directly run on the edge device.

There are 2 types of quantization. Post Training Quantization is easier, but usually does not provide results as good as QAT. 

- **Post Training Quantization (PTQ)**
    - PTQ maps the traditional FP32 activation space to a reduced INT8 space. This mapping has to be calibrated with your representative sample dataset in order to limit the information loss incurred during the mapping. 

- **Quantization Aware Training (QAT)**
    - Quantization Aware Training is a technique that constraints your weights to be already quantized during training, it should therefore offer highest accuracy on the edge model. However, it usually requires a decent float32 model, which is used as a starting point for the QAT. We only apply QAT on our best performing candidate models.

Datasets
^^^^^^^^
We have 2 datasets available:

1. Random positions and orientation dataset
2. Approach trajectory datasets

The 1) random dataset is split randomly following a standard 60-20-20 rule meaning 60% for training, 20% for validation, 20% for testing. Models are exclusively trained on the training split, and compared against each other on the validation split. The remaining test split is only used at the final stage, when the best candidates are evaluated in order to reduce data leakage as much as possible.

The final models are also evaluated on the trajectory datasets. We have 7 trajectories with different illumination conditions and split into two approach lengths [2; 100] meters and [2; 300] meters.

Future work: Additional experiments
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

We plan additional experiments that will give us more understanding of the problem at hand:

- Understand the amount of data required for a training set. Essentially find out when any new additional training images do not increase performance. This can be achieved by running experiments where validation set stays the same, but training set is limited by `x` samples where `x` is a number from a list of exponentially increasing numbers from `50` to `100_000` (or training image size). If the performance does not plateau then we might have to increase the number of training images.
- Train on non-optimal inputs (ie the output of the object detection, should increase robustness of the keypoint regression model)
- Differentiate between visibility categories for the keypoint regression network and penalize depending on the category
    - 1) Visible: in direct of line of sight
    - 0) Occluded: occluded by the object itself
    - -1) Out of field-of-view: when keypoints were cropped out

Object detection model selection
--------------------------------

The model selection for the object detection task was performed on two most promising models, namely FasterRCNN and FCOS. The first one is a two-stage detector composed of a Region Proposal Network (RPN) followed by ROI Pooling operation and finally terminated with regression and classification heads. On the other hand, FCOS is a single-stage detector with a single neural network based on a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) acting as the network backbone. It treats object detection as a per-pixel regression and classification task making it a much simpler architecture. Typically, FasterRCNN is attributed with higher accuracy (especially on smaller objects) on but also higher latency compared to FCOS, it also takes longer to train the network. Another important limitation of FasterRCNN is that it is not possible to do Quantization Aware Training, because of it's architecture. Nonetheless, we have performed various experiments to test the hypothetical higher performance of FasterRCNN compared to FCOS and we actually arrived at opposites results.

Model training
--------------

We trained each model separately and evaluate its performance individually. Deep learning frameworks trade-off
----------------------------------

The trade-off between PyTorch and TensorFlow (Keras) was initiated following the issues faced while embedding CNN models with Vitis-AI in DIOSSA phase 1. The main goal of the trade-off is to select the framework that is more likely to guarantee the successful deployment of CNN models with Vitis-AI.  
The evaluation of the two deep learning frameworks is included in the next table. The qualitative metrics were selected focusing on the target application, which is to be integrated in an edge device.

- **API**
    - **PyTorch 1.x**: PyTorch includes a sequential container and a subclassing option. Both can be mixed and nested and **used with Vitis-AI without limitations**.
    - **TensorFlow 2.x (Keras 2.x)**: Keras includes a sequential and a functional API, as well as a subclassing option. However, the Vitis-AI quantizer **only supports the functional API**, which itself **imposes some restrictions on the possible CNN models to be deployed**.
- **Edge friendliness**
    - **PyTorch 1.x**: PyTorch had **PyTorch Mobile (which has no footprint on edge deployment)**, but it was replaced (in version 2.x) with a **new toolkit called ExecuTorch (still to gain market traction)**.
    - **TensorFlow 2.x (Keras 2.x)**: TensorFlow has a significant lead in terms of edge deployment, with **TensorFlow Lite**, which **is widely adopted by the industry**.
- **Off-the-shelf models availability**
    - **PyTorch 1.x**: PyTorch is more widely used in academic environments, which reflects on the **vast catalog of off-the-shelf models available**.
    - **TensorFlow 2.x (Keras 2.x)**: The **catalog of off-the-shelf models in TensorFlow is smaller**, even more so if the single API supported by Vitis-AI is considered.
- **Custom models development effort**
    - **PyTorch 1.x**: The Vitis-AI PyTorch **workflow requires many subclasses for each specific purpose** (PTQ and QAT). **All the models embedded were done so without facing operator or quantizer/compiler limitations**.
    - **TensorFlow 2.x (Keras 2.x)**: The Vitis-AI TensorFlow **workflow requires the Keras functional API**, which has a more limited set of operations, but it **allows to fulfill all the needs with a single Python class**. However, **the limited set of operations combined with a more limited quantizer/compiler constitute a major roadblock**.
- **Speed (training and inference)**
    - **PyTorch 1.x**: PyTorch **always runs in eager execution, being slower for training and inference**. (Version 2.x apparently allows easy compilation).
    - **TensorFlow 2.x (Keras 2.x)**: TensorFlow **can easily switch between eager execution and compiled computational graph**, which **combines debugging easiness with significant speed advantage**.

TensorFlow is (in theory) the more edge friendly framework, and the well established TensorFlow Lite is the main proof, however the identified limitations in the Vitis-AI TensorFlow quantizer prevent the correct embedding of one of the selected CNN models and this issue was rated as showstopper. The Vitis-AI TensorFlow quantizer together with the required Keras functional API do not support a CNN model with shared layers, therefore PyTorch is the best and only option to successfully embed the CNN models required by the algorithms used in DIOSSA without having deviations from the original CNN models.

While PyTorch is slower (to train and infer) and less likely to be directly deployed to the edge, it has more off-the-shelf models publicly available. The nature of the CNN models (black box) make it very hard if not impossible to identify small issues in the CNN model definitions and the strategy adopted by LMO is to validate the results of the custom models against the off-the-shelf counterpart. Only after an exact match of the outputs for the same inputs is the development of a custom model finished. This validation strategy is only possible if there is an off-the-shelf model available, so the large catalog of off-the-shelf models is a critical aspect.

The observations made in this trade-off are valid for Vitis-AI 3.5 (the latest version at the moment of writing). Newer versions of Vitis-AI might require to revise the trade-off, but unless required to move to a more recent version, for DIOSSA ultimately it is possible to freeze the version. The choice of CNN HW accelerator, the DPU in an UltraScale+ MPSoC, comes with a set of associated libraries and tools all under the umbrella of Vitis-AI, so the developments in DIOSSA are dependent on the supported features of Vitis-AI and the development roadmap of Vitis-AI can have some impact on DIOSSA.

An alternative to PyTorch or TensorFlow already available is Keras 3. With version 3, Keras will go back to its roots becoming framework agnostic once again (supporting TensorFlow, PyTorch and JAX), which means Keras will make it very simple to change the backend framework with minimal to no changes in source code. Being framework agnostic could potentially allow to eliminate the drawbacks of the backend frameworks by allowing to select the best framework for each target application. Unfortunately Vitis-AI does not support Keras 3 yet, so at least for the moment Keras 3 is not a possible option.

Sensor fusion trade-off
-----------------------

In the literature [RD-1] we can find several sensor fusion methods, which could be pretty well fit into three plus one categories:

1. Early fusion. The fusion happens at pixel-level (or any other raw data) and methods vary on how to combine different sensory outputs together and create a fused image, which will be fed to the downstream computer vision task.
2. Mid fusion. Here first features are extracted (using any classic feature descriptor or the output of a deep neural network feature layer) and fusion happens at this feature level by combining those into a bigger feature vector, which will be fed to the remaining computer vision processing modules.
3. Late fusion. In this category fall methods, in which, each sensor is processed separately and provides its own decision (based on the task at hand, such as classification, keypoints detection, object tracking, etc.). The generated decisions are combined to form a final decision, following various approaches, most commonly using a voting scheme or interpolation.
4. Hybrid fusion. Finally the "plus one" method is a combination of early or mid with late fusion. Adding the late fusion can improve results by using information that might be lost during earlier stage fusion.

The figure below illustrates the three different architectures for sensor fusion.

.. figure:: images/sensor_fusion.drawio.png
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    Sensor fusion architectures

For the DIOSSA project, only visual and thermal infrared cameras are considered. For other applications fusion from other sensors could also be considered - Lidar, depth camera, event camera, or classical AOCS sensors such as accelerometers or star trackers.

While the visible camera collects more data thanks to its higher resolution, and potentially its 3-channels sensor for color cameras, the thermal infrared camera acquires the infrared radiation emitted by the target spacecraft as it generates heat from electronic units, making it usable in degraded light conditions, as for example in eclipse.

The same reasoning that led us to select a hybrid structure for the neural networks also applies for the sensor fusion: if both modalities (visible and infrared) are implemented separately up to decision making, it allows to better understand the performance of each of them and their limits. Late fusion also permits a more transparent decision by discarding information that would not reach a pre-defined confidence threshold. 

Aside from performance outcome, which has not yet been evaluated, late fusion presents the drawback that the processing power hungry NN inference has to run twice in parallel. At this stage, it is considered to be acceptable, and if successful, optimization techniques will be investigated.

While other fusion techniques might provide interesting performance upgrade, they also involve more experimenting, and for the reasons stated above it has been decided to only implement and test late fusion for this phase of the project.

Late fusion implementation
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

In the selected late fusion approach, we have two processing pipelines, one for each sensor, as illustrated by the arrows pointing up in the figure above, producing two separate 3D pose estimations outputs. 

The fusion is done at the decision level, meaning that for each timestamp, we select the estimated 3D pose from either the visible or the thermal sensor's output based on the *prediction_validity* flag. If both sensors provide a valid prediction, the result from the visible sensor is chosen. The final output will also include information indicating whether the result came from the visible or the thermal camera.


Simulator updates
=================

Inertial measurement unit (IMU) model
-------------------------------------

In order to provide an accurate IMU measurement model, a noisy gyroscope and accelerometer model is introduced.
The gyroscope model uses the angular rate of the spacecraft as ground truth where as the ground truth of the accelerometer model is chosen to be the acceleration of the spacecraft due to thruster firing.
The gyroscope model contains the angular random walk (ARW), rate random walk (RRW) and bias instability (BI). Further, a bias can be set.

The mathematical model is

.. math::

    \omega_{meas} =  \omega_{groundtruth} +  \omega_{bias} + \omega_{arw} +  \omega_{rrw}

For the accelerometer, the velocity random walk (VRW), acceleration random walk (AccRW) and bias instability are modelled. Also here, the a bias in the measurement can be set if desired.

The corresponding model is 

.. math:: 

    a_{meas} =  \frac{F_{groundtruth}}{m_{S/C}}+  a_{bias} + a_{arw} +  a_{rrw}

ARW and VRW model
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

The ARW and the VRW are modelled as a Gaussian white noise on the angular velocity and translational acceleration respectively.
Integrating over time leads to a random walk in the angle or velocity.

RRW and AccRW model
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

The RRW and the AccRW are modelled as a Gaussian white noise on the angular acceleration and jerk respectively.
Integrating over time leads to a random walk in the angular velocity or translational velocity.

Bias Instability
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Modelling the bias instability is not as trivial as modelling a random walk. 
To model the bias instability a pink noise model is chosen. 

Pink noise is modelled as a recursive IIR filter:

.. math:: 
    x_n = -a_1 x_{n-1}-a_2 x_{n-2}-a_3 x_{n-3}-...+w_n

:math:`w_n` represents a Gaussian white noise with a given standard deviation.

The filter coefficients are computed, using the following formula:

.. math::

    a_0 = 1

    a_n = \frac{(n-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}) a_{n-1}}{n}

Since the low frequency part of the Pink Noise model is of interest for the bias instability, the sampling frequency of the flicker noise generation is set to 1000 times the sampling frequency of the sensor.
Thus, the noise value is held constant over this time.

To ensure a smooth signal, it is low pass filtered. Here, a first order filter low pass filter with a cutoff frequency of :math:`{cutoff} = \frac{1}{\Delta t_{bias}}`

This gives the following filter coefficients

.. math::

    a_0 = 1   ~~~~~    a_1 = -0.99373647

    b_0 = 0.00313176 ~~~~~ b_1 = 0.00313176

Navigation filters
------------------

To ensure a consistent and more reliable measurement process, the output of the neural networks is integrated into a navigation filter.
During an earlier phase of this study, two Extended Kalman Filters (EKFs) were developed, each differing in terms of the required measurement
inputs and their underlying measurement models.

To evaluate the performance of these filters with new models, synthetic images were introduced, generating either detected keypoints or
estimated pose as inputs for the filters.

The graph below illustrates the relative position estimated by the EKF using quaternion and position data as inputs from the models.

    :width: 100%
    :align: center

The results show that the filter diverges for the initial measurements. Additionally, the output of the models contains a significant
number of outliers, leading to non-Gaussian measurement noise.

Using the EKF variant that processes keypoints as input yields the following results:

.. figure:: images/badVisibility_KPinput.png
    :width: 100%
    :align: center

    Bad visibility KPs as input

Similar behavior is observed in this scenario. It is important to note that the reduced visibility of the target satellite in this case
causes a notable increase in measurement noise. For comparison, a scenario with improved visibility was analyzed.

The following graph shows the estimated relative position using an EKF with pose data as input:

.. figure:: images/traj2_betterModel.png
    :width: 100%

In this case, the measurement noise is closer to Gaussian. However, the approximation remains insufficiently accurate, causing the filter to diverge.

Functional testing facility
===========================

For the functional testing of the RPO system, two facilities have been identified:

1. The IOSM Yard in Westcott (UK).
2. The GNC Rendezvous, Approach and Landing Simulator (GRALS) located at ESTEC (NL).

.. figure:: images/IOSM_yard.jpg
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    IOSM Yard

.. figure:: images/GRALS_Testbed.jpg
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    GRALS testbed


While the two facilities provide similar capability of simulating approach trajectory with two robotic arms in a dark environment, their visit highlighted some differentiating factors summarized in the table below.

.. table:: Lab characteristics
    :width: 100%
    :widths: auto

    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Criteria                                     | IOSM Yard                         | GRALS                                        |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Useful length for serving satellite (camera) | 17 m                              | 28 m                                         |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Payload capacity                             | Up to 120 kg                      | Up to 10 kg                                  |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Light source                                 | Fixed on tripod, moved manually   | Installed on 3rd robotic arm, automated      |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Control system                               | ROS, outputs in rosbags           | Matlab/simulink, outputs in text files       |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+
    | Operations                                   | From control room on second floor | In the same room, protected by laser barrier |
    +----------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------+----------------------------------------------+

While the greater payload capacity (of the IOSM lab) would make the mockup design easier, the other points (of the GRALS lab) would make the rest of the testing activities (preparation, execution, data processing, etc.) more flexible. Therefore, it has been decided to select the GRALS facility for the functional testing activities of this development phase.

Functional testing satellite model
==================================

For the functional testing of the RPO system, we have identified and modeled a telecommunications satellite located on a GEO orbit: Inmarsat-5 F4. 

.. figure:: images/Inmarsat_Artist_Picture.jpg
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    Artist view of Inmarsat-5 F4

Existing 3D satellite models were utilized to create a CAD model and drawings that conform to the lab's constraints:
- Maximum weight of 10kg on the robotic arms 
- Under 6kg there aren't any load factor issues with the robotic arms

The Inmarsat-5 F4's body (without the solar panels) is dimensioned as such : ``3.27m x 6.98m x 8.08m`` (Length x Height x Width). To fit the model into the lab and on the robotic arm, a scaling factor of ``12`` has been implemented on the design. The model main body is now dimensioned as follows: ``0.2725m x 0.582m x 0.674m`` (Length x Height x Width).

.. figure:: images/Manufactured_mockup.png
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    Manufactured and Assembled Mockup


The factor of 12 provides an effective balance between weight and feature precision in manufacturing. Weighing just 7.3 kg, the mockup does not impose significant weight limitations on the GRALS facility's robotic arms. The selected materials contribute to the mockup's lightweight nature. Each material listed below helps achieve this reduced weight:

- machined aluminium, used on structural parts and outer panels,
- 3D printed aluminium, used for features and antenna dishes,
- aluminium tape, applied on the panels to give a shinier optical feature,
- white matte plastic, used for the transmitter at the end of the main body (opposite to the large apogee engine),
- mylar, used on the uncovered parts of the satellite main body,
- printed stickers, used to simulate the solar panels.

Along with the material selection, the mockup's design features areas where material has been removed, primarily from the structural panels that form the framework of the main body. These structural panels help maintain the mockup's structural integrity when assembled while keeping its weight minimal. 

.. figure:: images/MainBody_structural_panels.png
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    Mockup Structural Panels


.. figure:: images/MainBody_under_assembly.png
    :width: 80%
    :align: center

    Main Body Structure Under Assembly


The solar arrays have been significantly shortened, as the satellite's total length under space operation conditions is 33.8 meters. As a result, even at a 1/12 scale, the solar panels would either not fit in the GRALS facility or would impose additional constraints on the robotic arms and the testing campaign.

Although the mechanical interface with the robotic arm can be placed in various locations on the mockup, the ideal position is on the face of the satellite body where the solar panels are mounted. In this position, the camera has an improved view of the key features of the satellite model on both sides of its body.
